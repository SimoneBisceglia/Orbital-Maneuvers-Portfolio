1. QUALITATIVE ACTUATOR COMPARISON
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1.1 Reaction Wheels (RW)
The Reaction Wheel architecture provides the smoothest dynamic response among the three configurations and successfully tracks the commanded trajectory throughout the entire simulation. Because the wheels operate with a wide torque margin over the maneuver demand, the spacecraft follows the quintic S-curve profile continuously across all three axes, with the dominant angular rate occurring on the Pitch axis (which executes the largest rotation). 

Once the active slew concludes, the angular rates settle smoothly onto the steady-state orbital tracking profile without residual oscillations. The electrical power profile is strictly confined to the active acceleration and deceleration phases of the maneuver, exhibiting a clean double-peak shape before dropping to near-zero maintenance levels during post-maneuver fine pointing. A minor steady-state pointing residual remains at the end of the simulation, driven primarily by uncompensated gyroscope thermal bias and sensor noise inside the proportional-derivative loop.

Assessment: RW provides the optimal combination of slew agility, continuous torque modulation, fine-pointing accuracy, and minimal steady-state power consumption.


1.2 Cold Gas Thrusters (THR)
The Cold Gas Thruster architecture matches the slew agility of the Reaction Wheels, demonstrating that a thruster-based system has more than enough control authority to execute rapid rotational maneuvers. During the active slew window, the thrusters drive the spacecraft toward the target orientation through a sequence of discrete, stepped velocity increments.

However, the system is severely penalized by its discrete On/Off (Bang-Bang) actuation logic once the main slew is completed. In the post-maneuver settling phase, navigation sensor noise and uncompensated gyroscope bias continuously trigger the controller across the switching deadband. This interaction traps the spacecraft, most notably along the Roll axis, into a persistent, high-frequency limit cycle (chattering). Consequently, the thrusters continue to fire intermittently for several hundred seconds after the maneuver has ended, wasting propellant without improving pointing accuracy, while the deadband itself leaves a noticeable static pointing offset.

Assessment: THR offers high slew authority for rapid or coarse maneuvers, but exhibits poor fine-pointing settling and prohibitive steady-state propellant consumption unless paired with a pulse modulator (e.g., PWPF) or an estimator for gyro-bias rejection.


1.3 Magnetorquers (MTQ)
The Magnetorquer architecture fails to track the time-constrained three-axis slew due to two combined physical limitations:
1) Insufficient Torque Authority (Primary Cause): At the simulated LEO altitude, the interaction between the maximum hardware magnetic dipole and the local geomagnetic field generates a peak torque roughly one order of magnitude lower than the nominal torque required by the 120-s slew profile.
2) Instantaneous Underactuation (Secondary Cause): Because magnetic torque is governed by the cross product between the coil dipole and the local geomagnetic field vector, no control torque can be generated parallel to the magnetic field line at any given instant.

As a result, the Pitch axis, which demands the highest control effort, suffers from severe phase lag, and the overall attitude evolution exhibits cross-axis divergence, failing to acquire the target attitude within the maneuver window and remaining far off-target by the end of the simulation. Attempting to compensate for the growing attitude error, all three magnetic coils immediately hit hardware saturation and remain clipped at their maximum power ceiling for over half of the simulation before slowly decaying into an offset equilibrium.

Assessment: MTQs are physically unsuitable for agile, time-constrained slews, operating in deep saturation without achieving the target orientation.

3. ENGINEERING INTERPRETATION AND CONCLUSIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The high-fidelity simulation results validate the classic subsystem sizing rules adopted in industrial AOCS/GNC design:

1. Reaction Wheels are the primary baseline for agile, three-axis precision pointing (|dq| < 0.01, equivalent to ~1.07 deg steady-state error under uncompensated gyro bias and sensor noise), providing continuous control authority with a ~10x single-axis torque margin at a fraction of a Watt. While unmodeled rotor momentum accumulation is negligible over a single 600-s slew, multi-orbit operations against secular SRP and drag torques require pairing the wheels with secondary actuators for momentum dumping.

2. Cold Gas Thrusters provide high instantaneous torque capable of matching RW slew times, making them effective for rapid slews, orbital maneuvers, or safe-mode recovery. However, coupling a Bang-Bang thruster law directly with noisy navigation sensors (Star Tracker noise and Gyro bias) triggers sustained Roll-axis limit cycling, rapidly depleting onboard propellant after the maneuver is over and leaving a static deadband pointing offset (~7.23 deg).

3. Magnetorquers fail the rapid slew primarily due to insufficient torque authority (~8x deficit against the Pitch slew demand), exacerbated by instantaneous magnetic underactuation (torque perpendicular to B). This drives the coils into continuous power saturation without completing the maneuver (~33 deg error). While geomagnetic field rotation along the orbit restores three-axis controllability over multi-orbit timescales, MTQs cannot execute fast time-constrained slews and should instead be paired with Reaction Wheels for continuous, propellant-free momentum unloading and initial detumbling (B-dot).
